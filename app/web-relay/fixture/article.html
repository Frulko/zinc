Paper that holds its picture

Electronic paper reflects the light around it, as ink on paper does, instead of sending light out as a screen does. A page stays on the glass without power: only a change costs energy, which is why a reader lasts weeks on one charge and why a picture that never moves is the best thing to show on it.

Each dot holds charged particles, black and white or coloured, that an electric field moves to the front or the back of a tiny capsule. Moving them takes time, so the panel is slow to change and keeps a faint ghost of the last page until a full refresh clears it; see how a refresh works for the waveforms that drive it.

Readers, shelf labels and signs use it because it reads well in daylight, costs nothing to keep a page shown, and is kind to the eyes over long sessions. Its limits are speed and colour: video and animation suit it badly, and colours are fewer and paler than on a lit screen, which is why pages made for it favour black text on white, large type and few greys.
